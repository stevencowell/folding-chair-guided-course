Week 11: Assemble in a sequence that preserves access and alignment

Adhesive shortens the time available for correction. The complete dry fit, clamp rehearsal and measurement plan must be ready first.

Theory 1

Assembly order is a design decision

Assembly should be divided into manageable subassemblies. A subassembly is a group of parts joined and checked before it becomes part of the whole product. The seat frame, backrest or fixed frame members may be completed separately so squareness, spacing and joint closure can be verified without the full chair obstructing clamps and tools.

The order depends on access. A joint that is easy to clamp now may become impossible after another rail is attached. Hardware should not be trapped behind a permanent joint. Surfaces that must slide during folding should not receive stray adhesive. Labels and orientation marks prevent a correct component being installed backwards.

A dry assembly tests the sequence as well as the fit. Lay out every component, clamp pad, square, measuring tool, damp cleaning cloth and protective block before adhesive is opened. Rehearse where each clamp will go and which measurement confirms alignment.

  1. 1
    Confirm the parts.

    Check labels, orientation, mating surfaces and approved design changes.

  2. 2
    Dry-fit the subassembly.

    Close every joint, check spacing and identify how clamps will be placed.

  3. 3
    Prepare the work area.

    Protect the bench, arrange tools and ensure there is enough uninterrupted time.

  4. 4
    Apply adhesive and assemble.

    Work methodically within the product’s open time.

  5. 5
    Clamp, measure and clean.

    Use controlled pressure, check squareness and remove appropriate squeeze-out.

  6. 6
    Leave undisturbed.

    Respect the stated clamp and cure times before stressing or machining the assembly.

Theory 2

Adhesive selection and the conditions for a strong bond

For many indoor timber assemblies, a suitable woodworking PVA adhesive provides a strong bond when the joint fits correctly and the surfaces are clean, sound and properly clamped. The manufacturer’s label and school procedure determine suitability, open time, clamp time, cure time and cleaning method.

Adhesive performs best as a thin continuous film between accurately fitted long-grain surfaces. It is not a gap-filling substitute for poor joinery. End grain can absorb adhesive rapidly and may require a process approved for the joint. Dust, oil, old finish or burnished surfaces can reduce wetting and contact.

TermMeaningPractical consequence
Open timeThe working period after adhesive application before assembly should be closed.Organise and rehearse first; do not begin a complex glue-up without enough time.
Clamp timeThe minimum period the joint remains supported under pressure.Do not remove clamps early simply because squeeze-out looks dry.
Cure timeThe longer period required to develop the intended bond properties.A joint may be unclamped but still not ready for heavy loading or machining.
Squeeze-outExcess adhesive forced from the joint line.A small amount can indicate coverage; heavy waste may show over-application.
G
More glue is not more strength. Joint geometry, fit, clean surfaces, complete contact and correct clamping determine the result.

Theory 3

Clamp pressure must close the joint without distorting it

Direct pressure

Position clamps so the force closes the joint rather than bending the assembly.

Controlled seating

Use only where approved; impact does not replace a correct dry fit.

Check 90°

Measure while the assembly can still be adjusted, not after the adhesive cures.

Use protective faces

A vice or clamp can bruise finished timber unless pressure is spread with pads.

Clamps should be positioned close enough to the joint to apply useful closing force. Protective pads spread pressure and prevent metal jaws marking the timber. Alternating clamps above and below a wide assembly can reduce bowing. Clamp pressure should bring shoulders and mating faces into contact; excessive pressure can rack the frame, crush fibres, bow rails or squeeze out too much adhesive.

Squareness can be checked with a square and by comparing diagonals. In a rectangular frame, equal diagonals indicate that opposite corners are the same distance apart. This is evidence of square geometry, provided the members are straight and the measurement points are consistent.

Theory 4

Costing converts quantities into evidence

A project cost is based on quantities and unit costs, not a guess at the final total. Direct material costs include timber, hardware and finishing products consumed by the chair. Consumables such as abrasives, adhesive and disposable application materials may be estimated by the amount used. Labour and overhead may be discussed separately depending on the assessment task.

Use consistent units. If timber is priced per metre, convert the component length from millimetres to metres and include a realistic waste allowance. Hardware sold as a packet may be costed per item by dividing packet cost by quantity, provided this is clearly stated.

Timber3.8 m × $9.50 per m = $36.10
Hardware and consumables$12.40 + $5.50 = $17.90
Example total$36.10 + $17.90 = $54.00

The values above are a worked example only. Use the school’s actual quantities and prices for the assessment.

Common costing errorWhy it is wrongBetter method

Week 12: Plan time around dependencies and quality—not wishful thinking

A realistic production plan includes the order of work, hold points, cure time, access to equipment and a buffer for correction.

Theory 5

Dependencies determine what can happen next

A dependency is a task that must be completed before another task can begin. Pivot holes cannot be located reliably until the frame parts have final length and shape. Finish cannot be applied responsibly until the chair has passed dry assembly and surface preparation. The full chair cannot be evaluated until fixed joints and hardware are complete.

A production plan should include estimated duration, equipment, material, responsible person and a quality check for each task. Some tasks can occur in parallel—for example, updating the folio while a glued subassembly remains clamped. Others form a critical sequence where delay affects the entire project.

1. Final dry fitConfirm all joints, hardware and movement.
2. Glue subassembliesWork within open time and check squareness.
3. Hold pointInspect joint closure after clamp time.
4. Surface preparationRemove residue and sand through the planned sequence.
5. Correction bufferAllow time for a fault, extra cure or finish test.

The critical path is the chain of dependent tasks that controls the earliest possible completion date. It is not necessarily the most difficult work. A short delayed task can be critical if every later stage depends on it. A useful buffer is placed where uncertainty is real, such as final adjustment, adhesive cure or finish drying—not added vaguely to every task.

Theory 6

Hold points protect the project from rushed rework

A hold point is a planned stop where evidence is checked before permanent or high-cost work continues. The chair project should have hold points before glue-up, after clamping, before finishing and before final submission. A teacher approval point may be required where structural or safety decisions are involved.

Before glue

Fit and sequence

All joints close dry, clamps fit, orientations are correct and the full assembly remains square.

After clamp time

Joint and geometry

Shoulders remain closed, rails are aligned and no component has shifted or bowed.

Before finish

Function and surface

Hardware, folding clearance, glue residue, scratches and edge quality are checked.

When the plan slips, do not simply compress every remaining task. Decide what can be simplified, deferred or completed in parallel without reducing safety or assessment evidence. Decorative extras are lower priority than structural completion, functional movement and a documented evaluation.

P
A good plan is updated. Record actual time, explain the cause of variance and revise the remaining sequence. Pretending the original plan is still accurate does not improve control.
