Week 13: The finish can only reveal the surface you prepared

Clear coating does not hide scratches, glue or poor shape. It often makes them more visible by changing the way light reflects from the timber.

Theory 1

Inspect before sanding

Surface preparation begins with diagnosis. Sanding the entire chair automatically can round crisp edges, change fit and waste time without removing the real fault.

Inspect under strong, angled light and use touch as well as sight. Look for dried adhesive, machine ripple, bandsaw marks, dents, torn grain, pencil lines, burn marks and abrupt transitions between curves and straight edges. Check moving faces and hardware recesses because finish build-up may reduce clearance.

Dried adhesive must be removed from the timber surface. A glue smear can block stain or clear finish from penetrating and may appear as a pale patch. Heavy residue should be removed with the approved scraper or chisel technique before normal sanding; attempting to sand a thick glue ridge can spread it and create a hollow around it.

Shape

Geometry first

Correct bumps, flats and mismatched paired profiles before using fine abrasives.

Surface

Defects second

Remove scratches, glue, dents and torn fibres without changing dimensions unnecessarily.

Finish readiness

Clean last

Remove dust and inspect the entire chair under consistent light before coating.

!
Do not sand pivot faces blindly. Excessive sanding can alter the hardware stack and create side play; finish build-up can do the opposite and create binding.

Theory 2

Abrasive grit, scratch pattern and sanding direction

Abrasive paper contains cutting particles. A lower grit number indicates larger particles and a coarser cut; a higher number indicates finer particles and a smaller scratch pattern. The starting grit should be only as coarse as needed to remove the current defect. Starting unnecessarily coarse creates deep scratches that require more work to remove.

Each grit must remove the scratch pattern left by the previous grit. Skipping too many grades can leave scratches that become visible after finish is applied. Sand with the grain on visible timber so any remaining scratches follow the natural fibre direction and are less conspicuous. A firm sanding block helps keep flat surfaces flat and prevents finger pressure rounding edges.

Theory 3

Select a finish for the chair’s use—not simply its colour

A finish changes appearance and can reduce moisture exchange, staining and surface wear. It does not make weak joinery strong or an indoor chair suitable for any outdoor condition. Selection should consider the product’s intended environment, expected handling, repair needs, appearance, application method, drying conditions, chemical hazards and maintenance.

Finish familyTypical behaviourUseful questions
Penetrating oilSoaks into the surface and emphasises grain; usually builds a thinner film.How often will it need maintenance? Is the product suitable for a seat and moving parts?
Clear film finishBuilds a protective layer on the surface and can provide a more uniform sheen.Will film build affect clearances? What ventilation and cure time are required?
Stain plus clear coatChanges colour before a protective coating is added.Will end grain or glue contamination absorb unevenly? Is a test piece essential?
Wax or polish systemProvides a soft sheen and feel but may offer less protection alone.Is it a final maintenance layer or the complete specified finish?

Always test the complete system on matching scrap prepared to the same grit. A finish can change colour, highlight end grain, raise fibres or react differently on different species. Record product name, batch or colour, surface preparation, number of coats and application conditions in the project log.

Read the product information

Suitability, spread rate, drying, recoating and clean-up vary between products.

Match controls to the product

Ventilation and the SDS determine whether respiratory protection is required.

Use suitable chemical PPE

Workshop gloves are not automatically chemical resistant; follow the SDS and school procedure.

Control access and ignition

Finishing areas require clear labelling, safe storage and controlled conditions.

Week 14: Apply thin, even coats and diagnose defects before correction

A professional finish comes from controlled preparation, product knowledge, environmental control and patience between coats.

Theory 4

Coating, drying, curing and de-nibbing

Application method depends on the selected product and school controls. Brushing, wiping or another approved method should produce a thin, even coat without flooding joints or pivot faces. Work in a clean, ventilated area and support the chair so all coated surfaces can dry without sticking to the bench.

Drying describes the stage at which a surface is no longer wet or tacky enough for the next handling step. Curing is the longer process in which the coating develops its intended hardness and resistance. A surface may feel dry but still mark under pressure or stick if the chair is folded too soon.

Final thin coatApplied only after the previous layer is dry, clean and suitable for recoating.
Light de-nib or key between coatsRemoves raised dust nibs and provides the surface required by the product directions.
First sealed coatReveals remaining scratches, glue contamination and uneven absorption.
Prepared timberCorrect shape, final approved grit, dust-free and fully inspected.

De-nibbing is the light removal of raised dust particles or fibres between coats using the specified fine abrasive or pad. It is not an opportunity to sand through edges or alter the shape. Remove dust using the approved method before the next coat.

The safest correction depends on the product and its state of cure. Do not add another coat simply because the first result is poor. Extra coating may trap contamination, deepen a run or increase clearance problems. Record the defect, likely cause and correction so the same process is not repeated.

Theory 6

Industry quality is built into the process

Timber and furniture workplaces use drawings, job sheets, safe procedures, jigs, sampling, inspection and traceable records to achieve consistent production. Quality assurance focuses on preventing defects through planned systems; quality control focuses on checking the product or process against requirements.

Standardisation

Templates, jigs, machine settings and approved work instructions reduce unnecessary variation.

Traceability

Job numbers, material records, finish batches and inspection results show what was used and when.

Communication

Drawings, handovers and fault reports allow several workers to contribute without losing information.

Quality checks

Inspection occurs at defined stages rather than relying only on a final look.

Maintenance

Sharp tools, extraction, clean equipment and fault reporting protect both safety and product quality.

Continuous improvement

Defects and delays are analysed so the process can be changed, not merely blamed on the operator.

When speaking with an industry representative or observing a workplace, useful questions focus on evidence: How are drawings revised? How are components checked before assembly? What happens when a finish batch is faulty? Which skills are expected from an apprentice? Good questions connect classroom practice to real production rather than asking only what machines are owned.
