Week 17: The portfolio should prove the work—not bury it

Strong evidence is relevant, authentic, labelled and connected to the brief, process or assessment criteria.

Theory 1

Build an evidence chain from brief to finished chair

A portfolio is not a scrapbook of everything produced. It is a structured argument showing what was required, what decisions were made, how the product was manufactured and how the final result was judged.

Brief and criteria

Purpose, user, constraints, safety and measurable success criteria.

Research and ideas

Alternative concepts, annotated sketches and reasons for selection.

Technical communication

Working drawings, dimensions, cutting list and approved revisions.

Production evidence

Sequencing, photographs, quality gates, problem-solving and safe practice.

Cost and sustainability

Quantities, actual costs, material decisions, waste and life-cycle thinking.

Evaluation

Evidence-based judgement against criteria, limitations and realistic improvements.

Evidence should be selected for purpose. One well-framed photograph of paired frame members against a template may prove more than five nearly identical workshop photos. Drafts are useful when they show development or correction; duplicates with no explanation increase file size but not understanding.

Portfolio integrity requires authentic work, accurate dates and acknowledgement of supplied drawings, sources and assistance where required. A neat layout supports communication, but decorative formatting cannot replace incomplete or misleading evidence.

E
Evidence answers a question. Ask what each item proves and which assessment criterion it supports. If it proves nothing new, simplify or remove it.

Theory 2

A caption should make the evidence interpretable

A weak caption merely repeats what can be seen: “This is my chair.” A strong caption adds information that the image cannot communicate alone. It identifies the stage, the process or decision, the quality evidence and the result.

For example: “Dry assembly before finishing. The specified washers were fitted between moving members and under each locknut. The chair completed the full open-to-folded cycle without frame collision, but the left pivot required a small teacher-approved tension adjustment.”

Photographs should be clear, relevant and ethically presented. Avoid including other students unnecessarily. Use consistent orientation and lighting. Detail images need enough surrounding context to show where the feature belongs, and overall images should show the chair open and folded.

Caption elementQuestion answeredExample wording
StageWhen was this?“After final dry assembly and before the first finish coat…”
Process or decisionWhat did you do and why?“The countersinks were prepared as mirrored pairs…”
EvidenceWhat can be verified?“Both R32 profiles matched the template and each other…”
Outcome or actionWhat happened next?“The parts passed the quality gate and were approved for drilling.”

Theory 3

Evaluation is a judgement supported by evidence

Description states what the chair is like. Evaluation judges how well it meets a criterion and explains the significance. “The chair folds” is description. “The chair met the folding-function criterion because it completed ten controlled cycles without binding or collision; however, slight side play at the right pivot indicates the hardware adjustment should be checked during maintenance” is evaluation.

1. Criterion

What was required?

Use the brief or a measurable design criterion.

2. Evidence

What proves it?

Use measurements, tests, photos, observations or records.

3. Judgement

How well was it met?

State the result, limitation and practical significance.

4. Cause

Why did it occur?

Connect the result to planning, material, process or control.

5. Improvement

What would change?

Propose a specific, realistic future action.

6. Transfer

What was learned?

Identify a habit or principle that applies to future work.

Honesty strengthens evaluation. A remaining fault should be named, its effect explained and a realistic response proposed. Claiming perfection prevents useful reflection and can conflict with visible evidence. Avoid vague improvements such as “be more careful”. State the action: “Use a drilling jig and compare the paired pivot centres on an alignment rod before countersinking.”

Week 18: Revise by recalling, connecting and applying

Rereading feels familiar but does not prove retrieval. Strong revision makes the brain produce the answer before checking it.

Theory 4

Retrieval practice and interleaving build usable knowledge

Retrieval practice means attempting to recall information without first looking at the answer. Examples include blank-page summaries, flash cards, labelled diagrams from memory and practice questions. The effort of recall strengthens access to the knowledge and reveals gaps that rereading can hide.

Interleaving mixes related topics or problem types. Instead of answering ten finish questions in a row, alternate between FEWTEL, bandsaw safety, drilling, costing and finishing. This forces the student to recognise which principle applies rather than relying on the order of the notes.

Less effective habitStronger alternativeWhy it helps
Highlighting whole pagesClose the notes and write five key ideas from memory.Tests retrieval rather than familiarity.
Studying one topic for a long blockMix safety, materials, drawing, production and evaluation questions.Builds discrimination between concepts.
Reading model answers repeatedlyAnswer first, compare, correct and retry later.Turns errors into feedback and spacing.
Memorising isolated wordsExplain each term using a chair example.Connects definition, cause and application.

Revision should be spaced over several sessions. A short return to a topic after some forgetting is often more useful than one long session. Keep an error log: record the misconception, the corrected principle and one example that proves the new understanding.

Theory 5

Command words determine the depth of the answer

Command wordRequired responseChair example
IdentifyName the relevant item or fact.Identify the fastener used at the pivot: an M6 countersunk bolt.
DescribeGive characteristics or a sequence.Describe the washer and locknut arrangement.
ExplainShow how or why, including cause and effect.Explain how washers reduce friction and timber wear.
JustifySupport a choice using relevant reasons and evidence.Justify a lower-hazard finish for the intended indoor use.
EvaluateMake a judgement against criteria using evidence and limitations.Evaluate the chair’s folding function and stability.
CompareShow important similarities and differences.Compare a fixed mortise-and-tenon joint with a bolted pivot.

A high-mark response also follows the scope of the question. If asked for two controls, name and explain two relevant controls. If the question refers to the folding chair, use chair-specific evidence instead of a generic woodworking paragraph.

Theory 6

Integrated answers connect stages of the project

Longer assessment questions often combine ideas. For example, a question about accurate folding action may require datums from Week 2, FEWTEL from Week 3, paired shaping from Weeks 5–6, pivot drilling from Week 7 and dry assembly from Week 8. The response should show how these stages depend on one another.

  1. 1
    Decode the question.

    Circle the command word, content areas and number of examples or controls required.

  2. 2
    Plan the logic.

    Write a brief sequence: principle → chair example → consequence.

  3. 3
    Use technical vocabulary.

    Terms such as datum, clearance, cumulative error and quality gate make relationships precise.

  4. 4
    Explain cause and effect.

    Do not list processes without showing how each supports accuracy, safety or function.

  5. 5
    Check coverage.

    Return to the question and confirm every required part has been answered.

A
Useful paragraph frame: “The principle is… In the folding chair this is applied by… This matters because… If it is ignored…”
