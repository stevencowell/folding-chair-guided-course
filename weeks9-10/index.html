Week 9: Improve the chair without weakening its purpose

A design feature is successful only when it improves the product while remaining safe, buildable and compatible with the folding mechanism.

Theory 1

Design criteria turn preference into a defensible decision

“I like it” is a starting reaction, not a design justification. A considered decision compares how well an option meets the brief, the user’s needs and the constraints of the school workshop.

Function

Does the feature support sitting, carrying, storage and smooth folding?

Strength and safety

Does it preserve fibres, clearances, stability and safe contact surfaces?

Appearance

Does it improve proportion, rhythm, line, colour, texture or visual unity?

Manufacturability

Can it be produced accurately with the available time, tools, material and skill?

Other useful criteria include comfort, portability, cost, maintenance, sustainability and repairability. These criteria can conflict. A deep decorative cut may create visual interest but reduce the cross-section near a load path. A thick backrest may be strong but heavy. The strongest design is usually a justified balance rather than the maximum score in one category.

Criteria should be measurable where possible. “Looks good” is vague. “The slat spacing is visually even, follows the width of the seat and can be produced with one template” is clearer. “Comfortable” can be explored through seat height, edge treatment, backrest support and a teacher-approved user test.

D
Design is decision-making under constraints. The feature must earn its place by improving the overall product—not merely add more work.

Theory 2

Structural, mechanical and ergonomic design zones

Not every surface is equally available for decoration. Areas near pivot holes, narrow frame sections, fixed joints and high-stress changes in section require sufficient timber around them. Removing material in these zones can create stress concentration or short grain.

Moving faces need clearance. A raised inlay, protruding screw, thick edge treatment or projecting handle can collide with another member as the chair closes. The full movement path must be traced, not just the open position. Finger-clearance and pinch zones should also be considered.

Ergonomic features relate the chair to the user. Rounded contact edges, appropriate seat height, back support and a practical carry point may improve comfort and handling. An ergonomic feature still needs structural and manufacturing evidence; an over-thinned edge may feel smooth but become fragile.

Loaded zones

Preserve timber around pivots, joints and narrow load paths.

2

3

Design development leaves evidence

Keep early sketches, annotations, comparisons and reasons for rejecting an option. These show that the final idea developed from investigation rather than appearing fully formed. When a change is approved, update every affected drawing, cutting-list note and production step. Manufacturing from an old drawing is a common source of rework.

Week 10: Communicate the design so another maker can build it

Technical drawing is successful when the views, line types, dimensions and notes remove ambiguity from manufacture.

Theory 4

Orthogonal projection separates shape into aligned views

Orthogonal drawings show an object using views projected at right angles. Each view communicates information that may be hidden in another. The front view normally establishes the main width and height; the top view shows depth and spacing; the side view reveals the folding geometry, frame overlap, seat height and pivot relationships.

The supplied chair drawings use third-angle projection. In this convention, the top view is placed above the front view and the right-side view is placed to the right. Features must align between views: a hole centre at a particular height in the front view must appear at the same height in the side view.

Front view

Width, symmetry, slat arrangement and vertical relationships.

Top view

Depth, spacing, thickness and alignment across the chair.

Side view

Folding geometry, pivot positions, seat height and member overlap.

?
Choose the view that shows the feature most clearly. A pivot-centre dimension usually belongs in the side view; slat spacing may be clearer in the top or front view.

Theory 5

Line types, dimensions and notes carry different information

ConventionPurposeChair example
Visible outlineShows an edge that can be seen in that view.Outer frame and seat profile.
Hidden lineShows an edge or feature behind the visible surface.A concealed trench or rear edge when needed.
Centre lineIdentifies the axis or centre of a circular or symmetrical feature.Pivot-hole centre and bolt axis.
Dimension and extension linesConnect a numerical value to the exact features it controls.Hole centre from an end datum; overall seat width.
Leader notePoints written information to a specific feature.“All holes 6.5 mm Ø” or “countersink other side”.

Good dimensioning provides enough information to manufacture the part without duplication or contradiction. Dimensions should refer to clear features such as ends, centre lines, faces, radii and diameters. Chained dimensions can accumulate tolerance; where alignment is critical, several features may be dimensioned from one datum.

The diameter symbol Ø identifies a circular diameter, while R identifies a radius. Units must be consistent. The chair plan states that dimensions are in millimetres and instructs the maker not to scale the drawing. The numerical dimensions remain authoritative when a page or screen changes size.

Theory 6

CAD improves revision—but it cannot detect a confident wrong number

Computer-aided drawing supports accurate geometry, copying, mirroring, layers, constraints and rapid revision. Layers can separate visible outlines, hidden detail, centre lines, dimensions and annotations. Snaps improve alignment, and geometric constraints can keep lines parallel, perpendicular, concentric or equal.

However, software follows the data entered. A perfectly constrained hole that was dimensioned from the wrong datum is still wrong. CAD drawings require an independent check against the project plan, approved sketch and actual component. A revision date or number helps identify the current version.
